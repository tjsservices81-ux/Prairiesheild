Chicago homes face a demanding mix of conditions: clay-heavy soil that swells and shrinks, wet springs, heavy summer storms and long freeze-thaw winters. Over time, that combination can show up as a damp basement, a new crack in a foundation wall, water pooling against the house or a musty crawl space.

This page collects the questions Chicagoland homeowners ask us most often. Each answer explains what the warning signs usually mean, which repair approaches are typically considered, and when a professional inspection makes sense. Use it to understand what you are seeing before you decide on next steps.

Foundation Repair

The most common signs are floors that slope or feel uneven, doors that stick or no longer latch, and gaps opening around window frames and trim. Outside, look for stair-step cracks in brick or block walls, and for a chimney or porch that appears to be pulling away from the house. Inside, cracks in drywall above doors and windows often appear first.

One symptom on its own is not always serious. Several of them together usually point to differential settlement, where one part of the house moves more than another. Because settlement rarely stops by itself, it is worth having a professional identify what is moving and why. Learn how we approach foundation repair in Chicago, from crack repair to steel pier underpinning.

Much of Chicagoland sits on clay soil, which swells when it is wet and shrinks as it dries. That constant change in volume means the ground under and around a foundation does not offer the same support all year. Wet springs, dry summers and drought years each change how firmly the soil holds the house.

Winter adds freeze-thaw cycles. Water in the soil and in existing cracks freezes, expands and thaws again, which can worsen small defects over several seasons. Poorly compacted fill beneath newer additions can also settle over time. None of this means every home will have problems, but it explains why cracks, sticking doors and uneven floors are common in the area, and why the cause should be identified before any repair is chosen.

Foundation Crack Repair

Be concerned when a crack is horizontal, when it has widened since you first noticed it, when it leaks or shows water staining, or when several new cracks appear in a short time. Horizontal cracks deserve the most attention because they usually mean soil is pushing sideways against the wall.

Thin vertical cracks in poured concrete are the most common type and often come from shrinkage or minor settlement. They are rarely structural, but they are the most likely to leak. Diagonal cracks that angle from corners, windows or doors suggest uneven settlement. A simple way to track a crack is to mark its ends and date them, then check again in a month or two. Our guide to foundation crack types explains each pattern in more detail.

A leaking crack is a direct path for groundwater. Professional repair seals that path through the full depth of the wall, rather than covering the surface where water can still travel behind the patch. For stable, non-structural cracks, that is typically done with injection that fills the crack from the inside face to the outside.

Not every crack is treated the same way. A crack that shows signs of ongoing movement needs structural attention, such as reinforcement or stabilization, before sealing alone can be expected to hold. That is why every crack should be inspected first. Caulk and hardware-store patches hide the evidence without addressing the cause, and they make it harder to see whether the crack is still moving. Basement Waterproofing

Chicago’s clay-heavy soil holds water after rain and snowmelt. As the soil saturates, that water presses against foundation walls and the basement floor, a force known as hydrostatic pressure. Over time it finds its way inside through cracks, through the joint where the floor meets the wall, and through porous concrete or block.

Drainage outside the house often makes the problem worse. Overflowing gutters, downspouts that discharge beside the foundation and soil that slopes toward the house all add water to the ground that is already pushing in. Warning signs include damp patches on walls, seepage along the base of the walls, a musty smell, and white chalky residue called efflorescence, which shows that moisture is moving through the concrete.

Interior waterproofing manages water that has already reached the foundation. The most common approach is interior drain tile: a perforated pipe in a gravel bed beneath the basement floor, along the footing, that collects water at the floor-wall joint and carries it to a sump pump. Wall sealing and crack injection are often part of the same plan.

Exterior approaches deal with water before it reaches the walls, through surface drainage such as French drains, regrading and downspout extensions. Neither is automatically better. The right choice depends on where the water comes from: roof runoff, surface grading or groundwater rising to the footing. Some homes need one approach and some benefit from a combination. Sump Pump Installation

A sump pump is usually worth considering when groundwater regularly reaches the foundation, for example when water appears along the floor-wall joint after storms, or when an interior drain tile system needs somewhere to send the water it collects. In much of Chicagoland, water tables can rise quickly during heavy rain, which is when a pump does its most important work.

If you already have a pump, watch for warning signs: it runs constantly or cycles on and off rapidly, makes grinding or rattling noises, shows rust or a sticking float switch, or the pit fills quickly whenever it rains. Age matters too, since many pumps are near the end of their life after seven to ten years. An inspection can confirm whether the pump is sized correctly for your home. Learn about sump pump installation and battery backup.

The most common reason a sump pump fails is not the pump itself. It is a power outage, and the storms that fill a sump pit are often the same storms that knock out electricity. Without power, even a new primary pump cannot move water, and a basement can take on water quickly.

A battery backup system sits alongside the primary pump and takes over automatically when the power goes out or the main pump fails. A high-water alarm adds an early warning before water reaches the floor. Whether a backup is essential depends on the property: how often the pump runs, how quickly the pit fills, and whether the basement is finished. For any basement that relies on a sump pump, it is one of the most practical safeguards available.

French Drain Installation

A French drain is a gravel-wrapped perforated pipe that collects surface and subsurface water before it reaches the foundation, then carries it downhill or to a discharge point away from the house. By giving water a better place to go, it reduces the hydrostatic pressure that pushes moisture through cracks, joints and porous concrete.

Exterior French drains work especially well for yard water and surface runoff, such as soil that stays soggy for days or water pooling against the foundation after rain. Water that rises from below and reaches the footing is usually better handled by interior drain tile connected to a sump pump. A proper assessment looks at roof runoff, grading and groundwater to decide whether one system or a combination is needed. See our French drain and drain tile systems.

Crawl Space Encapsulation

Encapsulation seals the crawl space off from ground moisture and humid outside air. A heavy-duty vapor barrier is fitted across the floor and up the walls, the seams are sealed and the vents are closed. Many homes also benefit from a dehumidifier that holds the space at a healthy humidity level.

This matters because air rises through a house. A damp crawl space sends humidity, musty odors and mold spores into the rooms above, and it can rot framing, rust ductwork and leave floors cold. Existing water problems must be fixed first: where water collects, drainage and a sump pump are added before anything is sealed, because a barrier laid over standing water only traps the problem.
